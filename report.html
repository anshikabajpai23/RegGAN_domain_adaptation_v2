Meniscus Segmentation - Progress Report

Before trying more fixes, I ran six checks to find out exactly where and why the segmentation model was failing, instead of guessing. Part 1 covers those checks, Part 2 the experiments they led to, and Part 3 places the result against the published literature.

Author Anshika Bajpai Baseline run_v7_replica_seed42, Dice 0.7780 Eval set 17 held-out real-PD patients
Part 1

The problem. The DESS→PD segmentation model plateaued around 0.78 Dice on real PD-weighted MRI. Rather than trying fixes at random, I first asked: is the error concentrated somewhere specific? Is it the model, or the ground-truth labels themselves? Six checks below answer that.

Why I retrained a "replica." I needed one fixed baseline to compare every check and experiment against, but I didn't have the original best run's validation slices or training numbers saved. So I reran the exact same recipe from scratch with a fixed random seed, and used that as my baseline going forward. It scored 0.7780, close to the original's 0.781 - the small gap is expected since a different random seed changes the result slightly.

Where this result sits. My best result so far (Part 2) is around 0.80 Dice. Against the published literature (Part 3, below), that's not too bad - comparable clinical work on small datasets lands around 0.76, and I likely have room to improve further once the ground-truth labeling issues from Check 4 are resolved.

Reading the screenshots below. In every Slicer screenshot in this report: red is the model's prediction, green is the ground truth (GT).

Check 1 of 6

Where in the volume is the error actually happening?

Why I asked this: a global Dice of 0.78 could mean uniformly-mediocre predictions everywhere, or it could mean the model is excellent almost everywhere and failing hard in one specific place. Those need completely different fixes, so I couldn't pick an approach without knowing which one it was.

What I did

Split every GT-labeled slice into three buckets - empty (no meniscus present), edge (within one slice of where the meniscus starts or ends along the scan axis), and core (interior slices, meniscus clearly present) - then measured what share of all wrong pixels fell in each bucket, on the 2 real-PD validation patients.

Conclusion

The error is not spread evenly. Edge slices - just 2 slices per patient out of ~30+ - account for 42% of all wrong pixels. Core (interior) slices, the bulk of the volume, account for only 25%. The model is essentially good at segmenting the meniscus once it's clearly present, and struggles specifically at the slices where the meniscus starts and stops. This pointed every subsequent check at the scan-axis ends, not at the model's general capacity.

Check 2 of 6

Does the model actually use neighboring slices, or ignore them?

Why I asked this: the model sees each slice as a 3-channel "2.5D" stack - normally the slice itself plus its two immediate neighbors, giving it some 3D context. If the end-slice failure (Check 1) is because the model can't see enough context along the scan axis, then giving it wider neighbor slices should help. But that's only worth trying if the model is actually using the neighbor channels it already has.

What I did

Compared three input variants on all 17 test patients: A - the same slice repeated 3× (no real neighbor information), B - the standard immediate neighbors (i−1, i, i+1), and C - wider neighbors (i−2, i, i+2). If the model is using neighbor context, B and C should both clearly beat A.

Conclusion

The model gets no benefit from its existing neighbor channels, and wider neighbors actively hurt. B (real immediate neighbors) is a wash against A (fake, repeated) - some patients up, some down, mean change ≈ 0. C (wider neighbors) is worse on 16 of 17 patients, mean −0.0225. The model was never learning to use the z-axis context it was given - it just ignores extra channels. This ruled out "wider stack, same architecture" and pointed toward either a different sampling strategy or a different architecture that has to learn to combine neighbors, rather than being handed them as fixed channels.

Check 3 of 6

Is the model memorizing real PD instead of learning from it?

Why I asked this: there are far fewer real-PD training slices than fake (synthetic) PD slices, so each real-PD batch gets reused roughly 21.6× per epoch to keep the two data sources balanced. That's a lot of repetition - enough that I suspected the model might be memorizing those specific real-PD slices rather than learning generalizable features from them, which would show up as good training Dice but no real val/test benefit.

What I did

Retrained with real-PD repeats capped to 1× per epoch (removing the 21.6× oversampling) and compared against the uncapped replica model, on both the 2 val patients and the full 17-patient test set.

Conclusion

The opposite of my hypothesis - the repetition is necessary, not harmful. Removing it dropped Dice by −0.038 on val and −0.029 on the 17-patient test set, both well above the ±0.0226 per-patient noise floor established in Check 5 below - a real, confirmed effect, not chance. The repeated exposure to real PD is acting as useful signal the model needs to counterbalance the much larger fake-PD dataset, not memorization. This closed off "reduce real-PD repetition" as a direction entirely.

Check 4 of 6

Do the ground-truth labels themselves have a problem at the ends?

Why I asked this: Check 1 showed error concentrates at the scan-axis ends. Before blaming the model for that, I needed to rule out the simpler explanation: maybe the human-drawn ground truth itself is inconsistent at exactly those slices - annotators stopping early, or being uncertain about where the meniscus actually starts/ends - in which case no amount of model improvement can fix it.

What I did

For every training patient, measured how a label's pixel count tapers toward the last labeled slice at each of the meniscus's two anatomical ends - the outer end (toward the joint capsule) and the inner end (toward the notch between the two menisci) - as a ratio to that span's peak pixel count. A ratio near 1.0 means the label stops abruptly at full size (a "cliff"); a ratio near 0 means it fades out gradually (a "ramp").

Conclusion

The ground truth has a systematic, non-anatomical pattern at exactly the slices where the model struggles. Outer ends stop abruptly at full size (median ratio 0.93, 11 of 30 spans exactly at peak) - a cliff, not a natural taper. Inner ends fade out gradually (median 0.39) - a ramp. This is consistent across all 15 training patients, which looks more like an annotation habit than biology. Part of the end-slice error (Check 1) is therefore not fixable by the model at all - it's inherited from how the labels were drawn. (See the checklist right after Part 1 for what's needed to settle this.)

Check 5 of 6

How much does Dice move just from random chance?

Why I asked this: every fix I test moves Dice by some small amount - but training itself is stochastic (random init, data order, augmentation). Without knowing how much two identical training runs can differ just from random seed, I had no way to tell whether a fix's effect was real or just noise. This check had to come before any of the fix comparisons in this project could be trusted.

What I did

Ran the exact same training script twice, changing only the random seed (42 vs. 7), and compared per-patient validation Dice.

Conclusion

Two identical runs can differ by up to 0.0226 Dice on a single patient, purely from random seed (mean absolute difference 0.0129). This became the noise floor for the whole project: any fix whose effect is smaller than this cannot be called "real" - it could just as easily be a different random seed. This check is why several later results in this project (fixes that looked promising but measured under 0.0226) had to be reported as "not yet confirmed" rather than "worked," even when the number moved in the right direction.

Check 6 of 6

Is the meniscus breaking into disconnected pieces?

Why I asked this: when visually inspecting predictions in 3D Slicer, I noticed the model sometimes predicts the meniscus as several disconnected chunks instead of one continuous curved band - especially on the synthetic (fake-PD) training data. Before treating this as a real failure mode worth fixing, I needed to know how much it actually costs on the data that matters: real PD.

What I did

On interior (core) slices of the 2 real-PD validation patients, measured how many wrong pixels belong to predicted "islands" that don't touch the ground-truth region at all (a genuine disconnected extra piece) versus every other kind of error (under- or over-segmenting the correct region).

Conclusion

Splitting is a real phenomenon, but not a meaningful cause of error on real PD. Disconnected components account for only 0.64% of core-slice error (26 of 4,054 wrong pixels) on the real-PD validation patients - what I'd seen frequently in Slicer was mostly on the synthetic fake-PD data, which has much thinner slices and behaves differently. This ruled out "fix the splitting" as a productive direction for the real-PD target domain and refocused effort back on the end-slice problem from Checks 1 and 4.

Part 2

Experiments - What I Tried to Fix It

Rule for every experiment below: start from the same untrained baseline checkpoint, change exactly one thing, retrain, then compare against the baseline (replica_seed42, Dice 0.7780) on the same 17-patient test set - isolating each change's effect instead of stacking unknowns. Every result is judged against the ± 0.0226 noise floor from Check 5: several results below look promising on paper but don't clear it, and I've labeled them accordingly rather than overclaiming.

Experiment 1 of 8 - Best result

Crop + Sequence, combined

Stacks Experiments 6 and 8, below Result: confirmed real gain
What it changes

The sequence architecture (Experiment 8, below) retrained on the same cropped, localized data (Experiment 6, below) instead of the original full-frame slices - zero new code needed, since the sequence model's dataset loader already expects the same directory layout the crop step produces. This tests whether the two best individual results - better localization and learned z-context - stack, or whether they're really the same underlying gain measured twice.

Result

17-patient test Dice: 0.7780 → 0.8057, Δ+0.0277 - above the 0.0226 noise floor. Roughly additive: crop alone gave +0.0130, sequence alone gave +0.0178 (sum ≈ +0.0307); the combined result lands close to, but a little under, that sum - consistent with the two effects being largely independent rather than double-counting the same gain. Both validation patients moved up together, the same pattern seen with the genuine TTA effect, not the seed-noise draw's push-pull pattern.

What the improvement looks like

Using the 2 validation patients (not the held-out test set, so this is safe to look at closely): AC0CEE9C24F2B7 improved the most overall, val Dice 0.751 → 0.797 (+0.046). Its clearest single slice is slice 18 - the same slice already flagged in the checklist above as "model under-segmented real meniscus": baseline Dice 0.489 → crop+sequence Dice 0.918 (+0.428). The prediction goes from a small fragment barely inside the true region to a tight match against GT. Honest caveat: not every slice improved - slice 19 got numerically worse (Dice 0.824 → 0.725), even though the prediction visibly catches more of the meniscus than before. The reason is false positives, not false negatives: missed pixels dropped from 20 to 11 (better recall), but the prediction also over-extended into background, raising false-positive pixels from 34 to 262 - a real example of the crop+sequence model sometimes trading precision for recall.

Experiment 2 of 8

P4 - Wider slice context + matched augmentation

Targets: Check 1 (edge error) + Check 2 (no z-context) Result: not confirmed
What it changes

Widened the fake-PD sampling stride to sample slices 4–5 apart (instead of consecutive), so the model's 3-slice input window spans more physical distance along the scan axis - direct response to Check 2 showing no benefit from immediate neighbors. Also equalized the fake-PD brightness/noise augmentation to match what the real-PD branch already used, removing a confound between the two data sources.

Result

17-patient test Dice: 0.7780 → 0.7856, Δ+0.0076. On a smaller 2-patient read this looked like +0.0090; with 8.5× more patients the effect got smaller, not more confirmed - the opposite of what more data replicating a real effect should do.

Conclusion

Below the 0.0226 noise floor. No confirmed improvement. Wider fixed-position context alone doesn't fix what Check 2 found - consistent with the model not learning to use neighbor slices no matter how they're spaced.

Experiment 3 of 8

P2 - Negative examples near the boundary

Targets: Check 1 (model paints tissue where meniscus hasn't started yet) Result: not confirmed
What it changes

Added slices adjacent to - and at the notch just past - the true meniscus span into the fake-PD training index as explicit negative (background-only) examples. The model had never seen "meniscus hasn't started yet" as a labeled case; this gives it that signal directly, rather than only ever seeing slices where meniscus is present.

Result

17-patient test Dice: 0.7780 → 0.7795, Δ+0.0015 - on the exact metric this change targeted.

Conclusion

Essentially nothing. Individual patients moved up to ±0.04 in both directions (a genuine per-patient effect exists), but it cancels out in the cohort mean. Negatives alone aren't enough to move aggregate test Dice.

Experiment 4 of 8

P1 - Capping real-PD repeats

Same experiment as Part 1, Check 3 Result: confirmed harm

This is the retrain behind Check 3 (see Part 1) - capping real-PD repeats from 21.6×/epoch to 1×/epoch. 17-patient test Dice: 0.7780 → 0.7489, Δ−0.0292, well above the noise floor in the harmful direction. Confirmed real harm - the only other confirmed-real result in this project besides the Crop + Sequence combination above. Chart: see Check 3 above.

Experiment 5 of 8

Checkpoint averaging & flip test-time augmentation

No retraining - inference only Result: suggestive, below floor
What it changes

Two changes applied purely at inference time, on the existing baseline checkpoint - no retraining needed. Checkpoint averaging: average the weights of the last two saved checkpoints (ckpt_best + ckpt_latest) instead of using one. Flip TTA: run inference 4 times per slice (original + horizontal flip + vertical flip + both), average the softmax probabilities, then take the final prediction - a standard way to squeeze out a small free gain with zero downside.

Result
Conclusion

Checkpoint averaging alone does almost nothing (+0.0034). Flip TTA gives a meaningfully larger +0.0175, and both validation patients moved in the same direction (unlike the seed-noise draw in Check 5, which moved them in opposite directions) - suggestive of a real, small, free effect, though not proven with only one noise-floor reference to compare against. Combining averaging with TTA adds nothing on top of TTA alone. Since this costs nothing and has no downside, I now apply flip TTA by default to every new checkpoint.

Experiment 6 of 8

Crop / localize

Targets: in-plane thin-structure detail Result: largest single-change gain, below floor
What it changes

Computed one fixed bounding box from where the meniscus ever appears across the training masks (+15px padding), then crop every slice to that box and resize back up to 384×384 before training and inference. The meniscus occupies far more pixels within the same input size - a 6px-thick structure becomes ~17px after upsampling - giving the network more resolution on exactly the thin structure it struggles with.

Result

17-patient test Dice: 0.7780 → 0.7910, Δ+0.0130 - the largest single-change gain before the combined experiment above (see ranking chart above).

Conclusion

Still below the 0.0226 floor on its own - not yet confirmed real. Open caveat: the box was computed from the 13 training patients only and has not yet been checked against how much of the 17 test patients' actual meniscus tissue falls inside it. Any test-patient tissue outside the box is an automatic miss baked into the box's geometry, not a model failure - that check is built (scripts/check_crop_box_coverage.py) but not yet run, so treat this number as promising but not fully validated.

Experiment 7 of 8

5-fold cross-validation

Measurement, not a fix Result: split choice doesn't matter
What it changes

Not a model change - a measurement. Pooled the 15 real-PD patients into 5 folds of 3, retrained 5 times holding out a different fold each time, then read each fold's checkpoint on the 17-patient test set. This asks: is the baseline's fixed 13-train/2-val split lucky, unlucky, or unremarkable?

Result
Conclusion

All 5 folds land within 0.0104 of each other - smaller than the seed-to-seed noise floor itself. Which 3 patients get held out barely affects test Dice; the original split wasn't accidentally favorable. It also implies random seed, not the train/val split choice, is the bigger source of run-to-run variation in this project.

Experiment 8 of 8

Slice-sequence architecture (ConvLSTM)

My own candidate - targets Check 2 directly Result: largest single-arch gain, below floor
What it changes

A genuinely different architecture, not a one-line change. Check 2 showed the model gets no benefit from neighbor slices handed to it as fixed input channels - so instead, this feeds 5 slices (i−2 … i+2) through the same encoder one at a time, then lets a ConvLSTM learn how to combine their features at the bottleneck. The decoder still works from the center slice's own skip connections, so fine spatial detail isn't blurred - only the deep semantic feature is temporally aggregated, and the network has to learn how much each neighbor is worth, rather than that being fixed by channel position.

Result

17-patient test Dice: 0.7780 → 0.7958, Δ+0.0178 - the largest gain from any single architecture or training change (see ranking chart above).

Conclusion

Still below the 0.0226 floor alone, so not yet "confirmed" by the same strict standard used to reject P4 and P2 - but it directly changes the Check 2 finding: giving the model a mechanism to learn neighbor weighting, instead of baking context into fixed channels, produces a real, if unconfirmed, improvement where wider fixed channels (P4) did not.

Part 3

Relevant Literature

What people do, in one paragraph. Almost everyone segments on thin-slice 3D DESS from OAI or SKM-TEA, where labels are dense and through-plane resolution is under 1 mm. On that data, a plain 2D U-Net gets 0.84 to 0.85, a shape prior or adversarial mask loss pushes to 0.87 to 0.89, and the 2019 IWOAI challenge showed the top four architectures are statistically tied. The recent direction is memory across slices (SAMRI-2) beating 3D convolutions - the same finding as my sequence result. The few papers on clinical 2D sequences with small N report about 0.76, with no cross-modality step at all.

Three consequences for the paper

1. 0.78–0.82 is not low for this regime. The one clinical small-N row in the table sits at 0.76 with native labels. My result matches it with zero native labels beyond 15 volumes - worth saying explicitly in the paper.

2. Nobody in this table faces the through-plane problem. Every high-Dice number above is on sub-millimetre slices. The core finding of this project - that 2D translation closes the in-plane gap but not the through-plane one (Part 1, Checks 1 and 4) - has no direct competitor in this literature.

3. SAMRI-2 is the closest comparison and the best citation. Memory across slices, evaluated against two radiologists. It validates the sequence-model direction (Part 2, Experiment 8) and shows exactly what a ceiling-referenced result looks like - the same thing the parked radiologist and Nikhil follow-ups in Part 1 are meant to give this project.
